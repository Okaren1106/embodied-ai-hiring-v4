# Initial editorial assessment and argument reconstruction

> **26 September 2026 scope addendum:** Subsequent qualification-scope analysis materially changes the recommended center of the paper. The earlier recommendation below treated narrow `Q` as “candidate-qualification articulation.” That label is too broad. `Q` is an **unconditional technology-specific qualification statement**: within GENERAL or REQUIRED it excludes choice, example, conditional and unresolved branches. A new nested measure `H` shows whether the target appears anywhere in a known GENERAL or REQUIRED branch; it excludes the separate PREFERRED metric. In robot-learning advertisements, PyTorch exceeds Python in mentions (83 versus 73) and H (79 versus 67), while Python exceeds PyTorch only under narrow Q (42 versus 23). Counting all recorded examples reverses the ordering (43 versus 65; Python-minus-PyTorch outer range −24.76 to −4.76), but an explicit-cue-only rule leaves the full-sample range crossing zero. The revised main argument is therefore that technology ordering depends on the qualification estimand and on which example forms an analyst admits. The 128 older readings do not exhaust this scope choice. `Research_Working_Manuscript.md` and `Research_Working_Supplement.md` supersede the earlier paired-articulation recommendation where they conflict. A separate implementation has reproduced the scope construction, without constituting independent human semantic validation.

## Editorial judgment

The v3 manuscript is unusually careful about denominators, unresolved readings and reproducibility. Its main weakness is not execution. It is that the paper makes a measurement correction carry the weight of a labor and HR contribution.

The current question—whether a firm comparison changes when a technology mention is separated from a direct qualification expression—is defensible but too close to a data-method note. The strict comparison threshold produces only three comparisons, all between Galbot and XSquare. The most memorable example, PyTorch, is a loss of directional support caused by adding two required statements. That example is useful as a warning about measurement, but it does not by itself explain an economically meaningful feature of recruitment or establish a general pattern of firm heterogeneity.

I would not recommend sending v3 to *International Journal of Manpower* in its current form. A likely desk-rejection reason is: technically transparent, but the substantive question and HR contribution are too narrow, the central firm comparison is local, and the paper offers no behavior or outcome beyond the text measure itself. The paper needs a different empirical object, not simply a stronger claim about the existing Galbot–XSquare example.

The strongest defensible object is **qualification articulation**: when a technology appears in an advertisement for a fixed task, does the employer present it as part of the work context or directly state it as something the applicant should bring? This question connects labor-demand measurement to recruitment communication without claiming that an advertisement reveals actual worker competence, screening behavior or hiring outcomes.

The paired v4 analysis makes this reconstruction materially stronger than the v3 firm-pair story, although the later scope analysis changes its interpretation. In the same 105 robot-learning advertisements, PyTorch is mentioned 9.52 percentage points more often than Python, yet Python has a 19.05-point higher confirmed unconditional-statement share. The new evidence comes from joining the two targets within advertisements and inspecting their joint states. Among the 66 advertisements that mention both technologies, 22 record a confirmed Python Q positive and a PyTorch Q nonpositive; one resolved record shows the reverse state. Fourteen are positive for both. Assigning all recorded unresolved states adversely leaves the Python-minus-PyTorch difference between +21.21 and +51.52 points in this common-mention subset.

The narrow-Q result is not an advertisement-weighting artifact in the current calculation. In the common-mention subset, the bounds remain positive under firm-equal weights (+17.04 to +47.08 points) and firm-by-exact-text weights (+17.24 to +47.15). In the full 105-advertisement sample, every company leave-one-out calculation also remains positive; the lowest adverse endpoint is +9.18 points. These are descriptive contrasts between two named technologies in a selected task and corpus; they are not technology effects and do not establish Q as the uniquely correct construct.

The other task pairs show why the claim should be about construct-dependent expression rather than a universal hierarchy. Among 48 robotics-software advertisements that mention both C++ and Linux, Linux is more often stated under narrow Q; the C++-minus-Linux range is −39.58 to −4.17 points. Among 17 circuitry advertisements that mention both Altium and the EMC/SI/PI family, the advertisement-weighted confirmed shares are equal, while alternative semantic readings and firm weights do not support a universal equality claim. The empirical regularity is that technologies visible in the same job text can have sharply different qualification relations, and the relation varies by pair and admission rule.

This paired reversal is the best candidate for the main empirical fact because it holds the advertisement, employer, task assignment and much of the wording environment fixed. It directly shows that technology visibility and candidate-qualification articulation can move in opposite directions inside the same recruitment text. That is more informative than observing that the set of direct expressions is smaller than the set of mentions, which follows partly from the definitions.

## Reconstructed research question and central claim

The paper should ask:

> When technologies are visible in vacancies for the same work task, which ones are directly articulated as candidate qualifications, and how much measured skill demand reflects technology visibility rather than qualification articulation?

A concise central claim, conditional on the remaining analyses, would be:

> Job advertisements contain two distinct margins of technology-related labor demand: whether a technology is made visible in the description of the work and whether it is directly articulated as a candidate qualification. In robot-learning advertisements, PyTorch is more visible, but Python is more often articulated as a qualification within the same advertisement. Treating mentions as requirements therefore changes the observed composition of employer demand, not merely its level.

Several words in that claim carry strict boundaries:

- “Visible” means explicitly mentioned in the captured advertisement.
- “Articulated as a qualification” means the saved text directly states an unconditional general or explicitly mandatory candidate qualification under the coding rule.
- “Employer demand” means public recruitment language in this corpus. It does not mean latent production need, actual screening criteria, the skills of hires, skill scarcity, wages or technology adoption.
- “Composition” refers to the relative profile of the fixed technology targets. It is not a causal response to a technology characteristic.

The phrase “translation from technology to qualification” is useful shorthand, but the paper should define it as an observed textual mapping. The data do not observe a manager deciding whether to translate a production need into a screening criterion.

## The substantive bridge to labor and HR research

The paper can join three literatures if it keeps their roles separate.

First, vacancy-based labor research treats job advertisements as granular measures of employer skill demand. Deming and Kahn (2018) show substantial skill-demand variation within occupations and across firms. Alekseeva et al. (2021) measure demand for AI skills from stated vacancy requirements. Daly, Groes and Jensen (2025) compare job-posting skill measures with skills workers report using after hire and show both the value and limits of the extensive margin. The present paper can add a prior measurement question: what textual relation is sufficient to classify a named technology as a candidate qualification?

Second, recruitment research distinguishes information about the job from information about applicant qualifications. Mason and Belt (1986) experimentally show that qualification specificity changes response intentions among people deemed unqualified. Later recruitment-message research likewise treats advertisement content as a signal that can affect applicant attraction and self-selection. This literature supplies the HR reason to care about the distinction. It does not license the present data to claim an effect on applications, applicant quality or selection.

Third, semantic vacancy research supplies the representation. Krüger et al. (2025) distinguish tools, work objects, alternatives and requirement strength. The v4 contribution should not claim to invent these semantic relations. It should show what happens to a labor-demand comparison when those relations are organized into two recruitment margins, paired within advertisements, and carried through recorded ambiguity.

The value added is therefore substantive plus measurement-based: the paper identifies an observable boundary between technologies used to describe work and technologies directly assigned to applicants, demonstrates a within-advertisement reversal in that boundary, and shows how keyword-based skill profiles can change when the boundary is respected.

## Argument architecture for v4

### 1. Introduction: lead with the paired reversal

Open with the empirical puzzle rather than with the general fact that job advertisements are widely used. A suitable logical sequence is:

1. Researchers and workforce planners often infer employer skill demand from named technologies in job advertisements.
2. A technology name can describe the work while saying nothing direct about what an applicant must already know.
3. In the same robot-learning advertisements, PyTorch is mentioned more often, but Python is more often directly stated as a qualification; the qualification difference remains positive under recorded unknown assignments, every admissible reading and every company leave-one-out.
4. The paper asks how much technology-related demand comes from visibility and how much from qualification articulation.
5. The contribution is to measure these two margins within the same advertisements and then assess firms and templates as sources of concentration.

The literature belongs after the question and main results. The introduction should not begin with GOJA or the coding protocol.

### 2. Conceptual framework: three layers, one observed transition

Use a short framework rather than a formal model:

`technology in the work or product context → published candidate qualification → actual screening and hiring`

The corpus observes the first two layers in advertisement text. It does not observe the third. A technology can be visible but not directly qualified because it is incidental, one option among substitutes, learnable after entry, assumed as baseline knowledge, or simply omitted by the writer. These mechanisms are observationally equivalent here. The paper should name them as interpretations that motivate future tests, not as findings.

Define the fixed-task base as the main denominator. For a target technology, the public direct-qualification prevalence can be written as an identity:

`Q/B = (M/B) × (Q/M)`

when states are determinate, with assignment ranges propagated when qualification is unresolved. `M/B` is the visibility margin. `Q/M` is the articulation margin among advertisements where the technology is visible. This identity gives the paper a clean organizing device. A symmetric decomposition can show whether a firm or technology difference in `Q/B` is accounted for more by visibility or by articulation. It remains an accounting decomposition, not mediation or causal mechanism evidence.

### 3. Data and design: center the paired observation

The data section should introduce the 1,654-advertisement corpus for provenance and selection, then move quickly to the 248-advertisement task sample and its two fixed targets per task. The primary design is paired at advertisement level. The robot-learning pair should lead because it provides the strongest current result. The runtime and circuitry pairs should show whether the distinction recurs, while making clear that C++ versus Linux and an application versus a method family are not symmetric technology comparisons.

The task-specific technology targets were selected after pilot reading and prior results. The study is exploratory. A confirmatory claim would require a later, separately frozen sample or a systematically defined larger target universe.

### 4. Results: move from the paired fact to concentration and fragility

The preferred order is:

1. **Visibility and articulation profiles.** Report `M/B`, `Q/B`, recorded unknown shares and `Q/M` for each task–technology target.
2. **Within-advertisement contrasts.** Show the paired state table, the Python–PyTorch reversal, common-mention results and the other two task pairs. Unknown and semantic envelopes belong here.
3. **Where the pattern comes from.** Compare advertisement weights, firm-equal weights, exact-template weights and company leave-one-out values. The current robot-learning results remain separated under all four views. These checks tell the reader whether a few employers or duplicated templates generate the result; they do not make the purposive corpus representative.
4. **Visibility–articulation decomposition.** Decompose differences in `Q/B` into the visibility and articulation margins using an exact symmetric identity. Avoid causal language.
5. **Firm comparisons as a secondary diagnostic.** Present the exhaustive pair analysis to show that firm rankings are measurement-sensitive. The old Galbot–XSquare table can illustrate the issue, but it should not carry the paper.
6. **Coding uncertainty and breakdown.** Separate recorded unknown assignments, admissible semantic readings and adversarial resolved-state changes. The new finding that four of the five supported firm reversals lose support after one resolved P/Z change, and the fifth after two, is a reason to demote firm rankings. It is not evidence that those states are actually wrong.

### 5. Discussion: HR relevance without invented behavior

The discussion can make three warranted points:

- Labor-market analytics that count names can mix descriptions of production context with public candidate qualifications.
- HR teams constructing skill inventories or applicant-facing specifications should preserve where and how a technology is stated, especially when a name appears as an example, alternative or duty.
- Differences in qualification articulation can affect the pool of people who perceive themselves as qualified, according to recruitment-message research, but this corpus does not observe that effect.

The discussion should not claim that firms “screen on” Python, prefer Python talent, face a Python shortage, train PyTorch after hire, or value Python more. All are plausible hypotheses outside the observed data.

## Most important empirical and editorial gaps

### 1. The outcome is public articulation, not latent demand

The present language sometimes slips from “direct expression” to “skill requirement.” That risks treating an unmentioned or contextual technology as unneeded. The revised paper should use “publicly articulated qualification” as the outcome and reserve “required” for the explicit `R` category. `Q` also includes ordinary unconditional qualification language.

### 2. The paired result is strong internally but narrow externally

Pairing removes many between-advertisement differences, but it does not turn Python and PyTorch into exchangeable treatments. Python is a programming language; PyTorch is a framework. A positive within-advertisement contrast says how these two targets are written in this task sample. It cannot identify an effect of generality, portability, scarcity, maturity or trainability. Those mechanisms require more targets and a target-level design.

### 3. Target choice is post-pilot and outcome-aware

The six targets were fixed before the current union analysis but after pilot reading and earlier results. That is acceptable for an exploratory paper if disclosed, but it weakens any claim that the Python–PyTorch contrast was discovered under an outcome-blind protocol. A future wave with a frozen target set would be the strongest extension. A broader existing-data analysis is useful only if it applies one consistent qualification relation to an outcome-blind technology universe; adding loosely comparable labels for sample size would make the paper weaker.

### 4. Company and template dependence must be visible

The same companies contribute many advertisements, and exact duty templates can duplicate the work description. Company leave-one-out, firm-equal and exact-template weights are essential. They diagnose concentration but do not create a representative population. Standard errors clustered by firm would not cure purposive firm selection or a small number of clusters, so the paper should favor transparent finite-corpus contrasts and sensitivity ranges.

### 5. The current source diagnostic is not validation

The 105-pair model rereading is stratified around earlier states and has no independent human gold standard. Match rates cannot be interpreted as accuracy. Semantic envelopes address known disagreements; they do not cover undetected P/Z error. The adversarial breakdown analysis is valuable because it states how many resolved changes would erase a conclusion, but it remains distance-to-failure rather than an error estimate.

### 6. No applicant, hiring or worker outcomes

The study cannot test whether qualification wording changes applications, applicant quality, interviews, hires, wages, retention or later skill use. The HR contribution must be about recruitment communication and measurement. Stronger behavioral claims require matched applicant or hire data, an experiment, or at least repeated changes in posting language tied to outcomes.

### 7. Cross-country and language comparisons are not identified

Headquarters, language, source platform, employer and technology mix are entangled. Country or language regressions would look more ambitious but would not answer a credible question with this sample. They should not be added as headline analyses.

### 8. Required versus general language has too little independent support

Only five advertisement–technology pairs are `R`-only additions to the union. They are important for defining `Q` and for the PyTorch firm example, but they are too few to support a general theory of mandatory versus ordinary qualification wording. Keep `G/R` distinctions in measurement and supplemental results.

## Claims that the existing data can test

The following propositions are empirical and feasible without inventing a hiring outcome:

1. **Paired articulation contrast.** Within advertisements assigned to the same task, the two fixed technologies have different mention and direct-qualification profiles. Report joint states and assignment bounds, not only marginal percentages.
2. **Visibility–articulation reversal.** A technology can be more visible yet less often articulated as a qualification within the same advertisements. The Python–PyTorch result currently supports this claim for robot-learning advertisements.
3. **Common-mention contrast.** Among advertisements mentioning both targets, one technology can be more directly articulated than the other. This narrows the comparison to a shared visibility set and currently remains positive for Python versus PyTorch.
4. **Concentration.** The robot-learning paired contrast survives equal firm weighting, exact-template weighting and every company leave-one-out in the current outputs. Report the exact ranges and the 22-versus-2 discordant joint-state count. Do not turn these deterministic checks into claims of population precision.
5. **Accounting source of differences.** Differences in fixed-base direct-qualification prevalence can be decomposed into technology visibility and articulation among mentions. The decomposition is exact and descriptive.
6. **Ranking sensitivity.** Within-task firm rankings based on mentions can differ from rankings based on direct qualification expressions. The claim should report how many comparisons have bounds-supported changes and how easily those classifications break under resolved-state perturbations.
7. **Semantic stability.** The paired result and any decomposition can be evaluated across the 128 saved admissible readings and recorded unknown assignments. Stability applies only to that defined ambiguity set.

Claims the data cannot test include: technology causes a skill requirement; applicants self-select in response; explicit qualifications are actually used to screen; one skill is more valuable, scarce, portable or difficult; embodied-AI firms in general follow the observed pattern; or national hiring systems explain firm differences.

## Red-team objections the paper must answer

**“Of course direct qualifications are fewer than mentions; `Q` is defined as a subset of `M`.”**  
Correct. The subset relation and aggregate level gap are not discoveries. The nontrivial result is that the relative technology profile reverses within the same advertisements and remains separated under recorded ambiguity. The paper must say this explicitly.

**“Python versus PyTorch is an apples-to-oranges comparison.”**  
It is a contrast between a language and a framework, not an estimate of a technology attribute. The response is to limit the claim to how two technologies in the same robot-learning advertisements are articulated, then test whether analogous visibility–articulation differences appear in the other task pairs. A theory of language-versus-framework specificity requires a larger target-level sample.

**“Not stating PyTorch as a qualification could mean it is assumed, not unimportant.”**  
Yes. That is why the outcome is public articulation. The paper should never translate `Z` into absence of employer demand or skill use.

**“The result may be one firm’s template.”**  
Firm leave-one-out, firm-equal and exact-template analyses directly address this concentration concern. Their purpose is to show the distribution of the finite-corpus pattern, not population inference.

**“The coding is model-produced and has no gold standard.”**  
This is the most serious validity and venue problem. Saved quotes, rule-based adjudication and semantic bounds help, but they do not replace independent human row-level coding. The paper must accurately report provenance, and a full human verification path may be necessary for submission.

2. **Technology Mentions and Candidate Qualifications in Embodied AI Hiring**

## Venue-policy barrier

Emerald’s current publishing-ethics page states that generative-AI copywriting of any part of a submission, including an abstract or literature review, is not permitted. Its narrative policy also states that generating, manipulating or reporting research data and results with an LLM is not permitted, while transparent use to aid analysis and copy-edit author-created text is allowed. The policy’s AI-usage table may describe some data-generation use differently; that apparent internal tension requires visual confirmation and publisher interpretation. This memo therefore does not declare the current analytical data ineligible. The copywriting restriction is nevertheless clear enough that a newly generated manuscript must remain an internal research work product rather than be represented as submission-ready author prose.

Disclosure alone does not necessarily cure a prohibited use. Nor can AI-written text be relabelled as author-written. Until the publisher or journal gives written guidance on the exact workflow, all v4 drafting should remain an internal research work product. Depending on that guidance, a credible IJM path could include:

1. a documented human, row-level review of the analytical qualification states, with disagreements and changes preserved rather than silently overwriting the model-assisted layer;
2. deterministic scripts to compute exhibits from that human-verified layer;
3. a manuscript independently composed by the human author, after which AI may be used only for permitted copy-editing with documentation; and
4. direct confirmation from the editorial office that the earlier AI-assisted analytical development does not make the submission ineligible.

These are contingency options, not requirements already imposed by Emerald. If the publisher interprets the classification workflow as prohibited generation of research data or results, the practical choices may be to rebuild the relevant evidence without generative AI or to select a journal whose policy permits transparent AI-assisted text analysis. This venue issue is independent of the scientific merits of the paired design.

## Recommendation for the next lock

Before treating any internal manuscript draft as stable, lock four facts:

1. the paired state tables and bounds for all three task pairs, including common-mention subsets;
2. independent verification of the firm-equal, exact-template and company leave-one-out results now produced;
3. the exact visibility–articulation decomposition with unknown and semantic envelopes; and
4. a venue-compliant validation and authorship plan.

The current weighted and leave-one-out outputs support replacing the Galbot–XSquare example with the Python–PyTorch joint-state contrast as the paper’s center, subject to independent verification. Firm comparisons should become a secondary demonstration of why measurement choice matters. If later verification changes that conclusion, the paper must follow the verified result rather than preserve the stronger narrative.
